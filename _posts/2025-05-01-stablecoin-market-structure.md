## Money Today

Ultimately, people want money that is interest-bearing, safe, and sound. While bank deposits offer safety and soundness, protected by institutional guarantees such as deposit insurance that insure deposits up to $250k, they offer little to no yield. As of April 2025, the national average interest rate for checking accounts in the U.S. was ~0.07%. Comparatively, the federal funds rate currently sits at ~4.3%. With current inflation rates at ~2.4%, bank depositors earn a negative real return on their balances. While money market funds offer interest in-line with the federal funds rate, they are not fit for payments because they are uninsured securities that lack connectivity to a real-time payment system.

Our current monetary system fails because there is no money that simultaneously meets the criteria of being interest-bearing, safe, and sound, forcing individuals to compromise on at least one critical dimension when choosing where to store their wealth.

How are banks able to get away with paying 0.07% on checking accounts when the fed funds rate is greater than 4%?

Banks in the U.S. have a monopoly on payments because they are the only institutions with permitted access to Federal Reserve payment rails (Fedwire, FedNow, etc). Whenever a nonbank institution wants to access the U.S. payment system, they must do so through a bank’s Federal Reserve master account — the account that provides banks with the ability to settle with other banks in central bank money. Through banks’ gatekeeping of the payment system, reinforced by deposit insurance, they are able to get away with paying near zero to depositors.

While banks in the U.S. maintain a near monopoly over access to America’s core payments infrastructure, stablecoins offer a radically different model -- instant, open, and global dollar payments for anyone with an internet connection and a wallet. However, where stablecoins excel as an instant, open, and global payment rail, they lack in their connectivity to the traditional system. Specifically, stablecoins lack the ability to settle payments in central bank money, an institutional requirement for any settlement method aiming to compete with incumbent networks on legitimacy, trust, and reliability.

## Stablecoin Market Structure is Broken

Traditional payment networks settle in central bank money because central bank money provides transaction finality, safety, and systemic stability. As a direct liability of the central bank, central bank money is considered the lowest risk form of money and eliminates the counterparty and credit risks associated with privately-issued forms of money such as stablecoins and commercial bank deposits.

**While many today believe that stablecoin transfers offer real-time settlement, in reality, stablecoin transfers are a real-time transfer of liability from sender to recipient, rather than true and final settlement**. If we want stablecoins to compete with bank settlement rails on feature parity and user experience, we need to prepare for a future in which consumers and businesses place varying degrees of trust across different stablecoin issuers, just as consumers today choose bank providers based on perceived safety, reputation, and cost. It’s unacceptable that today’s stablecoin market structure effectively forces consumers and businesses to hold liabilities issued by institutions they may neither know nor trust, leaving them exposed to issuer-related risks out of their control.

Imagine you’re a customer of Bank of America and someone from the fictitious Fourth Bank of Kentucky sends you money. As a Bank of America customer, would you feel comfortable accepting and storing your wealth in liabilities issued by the Fourth Bank of Kentucky? Definitely not! You’re a Bank of America customer, there’s trust established between you and Bank of America because you know them, and they know you. That trusted relationship doesn’t exist between you and the Fourth Bank of Kentucky.

However, this is exactly how the stablecoin market works today. When someone sends you a stablecoin, you’re forced to accept that stablecoin, even if you don’t know or trust the issuer. Just as consumers wouldn’t feel comfortable accepting liabilities from an unfamiliar bank (i.e. The Fourth Bank of Kentucky), consumers should not feel comfortable accepting a stablecoin from an unfamiliar issuer. To gain legitimacy as a payment rail, stablecoins must evolve towards a model that respects consumer preferences across different issuers through the introduction of a settlement mechanism for inter-stablecoin payments.

## Connecting Stablecoins to the Traditional System

In order for stablecoins to move towards a model that respects consumer preferences for different issuers, there needs to exist an inter-stablecoin settlement infrastructure (i.e. a clearinghouse) to clear and settle obligations across stablecoin issuers. This clearinghouse would reliably exchange stablecoins at par (without relying on secondary market liquidity), help users accept stablecoins issued by institutions they know and trust, and transition stablecoin payments from a real-time shift in liabilities to real-time settlement. Real-time stablecoin settlement, however, is only achievable in practice once stablecoin issuers agree upon a standard settlement instrument to settle obligations with one another in. But one problem remains: how do we achieve stablecoin payment settlement finality without access to central bank money?

As mentioned previously, banks maintain a monopoly over access to core payments infrastructure in the U.S., meaning they are the only institutions with the ability to settle in central bank money. Stablecoin issuers *could* rely on banks to provide them access to the U.S. payment system, but doing so would be ill advised with many banks [preparing](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2025/02/26/bank-of-america-ceo-says-bank-will-likely-launch-its-own-stablecoin) to launch competitive stablecoin or tokenized deposit products once regulation permits it. While there are [rumors](https://www.wsj.com/finance/currencies/crypto-knocks-on-the-door-of-a-banking-world-that-shut-it-out-082b3968) swirling that some stablecoin issuers such as Circle and Paxos are applying for trust bank charters, it is unclear whether Federal Reserve master accounts would be granted with approval. Given the Federal Reserve’s historical reluctance to grant nonbanks with master accounts, it is safe to assume that precedent won’t change any time soon.

What, then, would the ideal inter-stablecoin settlement instrument look like?  In order to match the 24/7, real-time nature of stablecoin liabilities, the asset used to facilitate inter-stablecoin settlement should also, naturally, be 24/7 and real-time.

The largest USD-denominated stablecoins by market cap, USDT (Tether) and USDC (Circle), primarily maintain their reserves in high-quality, short-duration instruments such as U.S. Treasury bills and money market funds to preserve liquidity, stability, and confidence. ~99% of USDT’s reserves are concentrated in U.S. Treasury bills, repurchase agreements, and money market funds, and ~88% of USDC’s reserves are concentrated in the Circle Reserve Fund, an SEC-registered government money market fund managed by BlackRock.

While MMFs appear to be an ideal candidate as a stablecoin settlement asset due to their liquidity, stability, and interest-bearing profile, as well as their wide adoption as collateral, traditional MMFs do not satisfy the 24/7 and real-time requirement we are looking for, given they live on archaic securities settlement systems that are slow and inefficient. However, tokenized MMFs offer a compelling solution by imbuing traditional MMFs with real-time, 24/7 transfer capabilities.

Tokenized MMFs are already beginning to gain traction as a settlement instrument within the digital asset economy. Last month, Tassat, Arca, and tZERO announced a joint venture called [Lynq](https://lynq.network/), an institutional-grade digital asset settlement network that leverages Arca’s tokenized MMF $TFND as a settlement instrument. Also last month, the DTCC, the post-trade market infrastructure utility, [announced](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/dtcc-to-launch-tokenized-collateral-platform/) a tokenized collateral management platform that allows for tokenized MMFs to be used as collateral and settled instantly across financial market participants. The [CME](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/cme-to-trial-tokenization-on-googles-new-universal-ledger-dlt/), [CFTC](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/cftc-unveils-plans-for-tokenized-collateral-pilot/), and [ICE](https://www.ledgerinsights.com/nyse-owner-ice-to-explore-usdc-stablecoin-tokenized-fund-for-capital-markets/) (the owner of the New York Stock Exchange) are also in the midst of piloting tokenized collateral for margining use-cases.

While tokenized MMFs do not offer the same safety, soundness, and counterparty risk guarantees that central bank money nor commercial bank deposits do, they currently represent the closest *tokenized* liability available to central bank money due to their high liquidity, stability, and minimal credit risk. As the financial system increasingly moves toward “[tokenizing every asset](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/04/12/tokenization-stock-bond-real-estate-trading-market-coming-blackrock.html),” I expect that other forms of regulated liabilities such as commercial bank deposits and even direct liabilities of the central bank to become tokenized. When that time comes, it’s possible that tokenized MMFs get displaced as the preferred settlement instrument for USD-denominated stablecoin payments in favor of something that is closer in nature to central bank money.

In the meanwhile, asset managers, through the issuance of tokenized MMFs, have a once in a generation opportunity at disintermediating banks by collectively becoming the de facto central bank ledger for USD-denominated stablecoins. However, because money is a social invention, it will take collective human agreement and belief that tokenized MMFs are a suitable alternative to central bank money for this bold vision to play out.

## Conclusion

Although robust regulatory safeguards, such as strict liquidity requirements, diversification mandates, and stress testing, are already in place to prevent tokenized MMFs from breaking the buck, their adoption as a quasi-central bank money replacement faces one significant barrier — the lack of deposit-like insurance or protection mechanisms. Unlike bank deposits, which offer FDIC insurance, tokenized MMF investors bear credit and market risks. This lack of absolute protection reduces consumer and institutional confidence, limiting tokenized MMFs’ viability as a universally trusted settlement instrument.

On the bright side, the history of U.S. banking offers a potential roadmap for addressing this issue. Before federal deposit insurance was introduced, banks managed deposit risk through mutualized, privately administered systems such as state-level insurance funds and clearinghouse associations that pooled resources to act as the lender of last resort during financial panics. More recently, when a prominent money market fund, the Reserve Primary Fund, "broke the buck" in the wake of the Great Financial Crisis, the Treasury created an insurance program (analogous to FDIC deposit insurance) that protected investors in money market funds.

Tokenization is quickly changing the future of money. If tokenized MMFs become the primary settlement asset for USD-denominated stablecoins, access to the U.S. payment system could expand beyond institutions with a Fed master account to anyone able to buy and hold a tokenized MMF, which would break banks’ current monopoly over U.S. payment rails. Democratizing payment infrastructure in this way would stimulate competition, reduce costs, and accelerate innovation, enabling more consumers and businesses worldwide to participate in, benefit from, and build on the U.S. financial system.
